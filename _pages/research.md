## Circumstellar Dust Around Extragalactic Red Supergiants with JWST

My 2026 summer research project through the [CIERA](https://ciera.northwestern.edu) [Research Experience for Undergraduates (REU) program](https://sites.northwestern.edu/cierareu/) at [Northwestern University](https://Northwestern.edu) focused on analyzing and constraining circumstellar dust properties around red supergiant (RSG) supernova progenitor candidates in M51 and M82. Working with Dr. Charlie Kilpatrick and Aswin Suresh, I used observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) to investigate the fraction of RSGs better explained by graphite-dominated versus silicate-dominated circumstellar dust models.

As an extension of their research identifying and classifying RSGs using JWST Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam) observations, I incorporated images from the Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) alongside NIRCam to examine RSG circumstellar dust emission across a broader wavelength range. This involved using Python and modifying existing pipelines to align the MIRI and NIRCam datasets for analysis.

In M51, the RSG sample consists of 3,360 stars, of which 63.9% were found to be silicate-dominated and 36.1% were graphite-dominated. Similarly, in M82, 68.0% of a sample of 303 RSGs were found to be silicate-dominated, while 32.0% were graphite-dominated. The prevalence of silicate-dominated sources in both galaxies is consistent with the oxygen-rich environments expected around RSGs, where C/O < 1 favors the formation of silicate dust. Silicate dust is therefore preferred in both galaxies. 

However, the number of RSGs best represented by graphite models is somewhat unexpected, as graphite-dominated CSMs may indicate periods of dredge-up or unusual chemical evolution over their mass-loss histories, given that RSGs are generally expected to be silicate-dominated.

The difference between the silicate and graphite fractions in M51 and M82 is approximately four percentage points. Similar composition ratios between the two galaxies despite their diverse star-forming environments suggest that the dust chemistry of RSG CSM may be more strongly determined by stellar evolutionary processes than by differences in host-galaxy properties.

By placing observational constraints on RSG circumstellar dust composition, we can refine our understanding of late-stage massive stellar evolution and the physical properties of Type II supernova progenitors.

## Superluminous Supernovae and Roman Space Telescope Detectability

In my [transients research group](https://sites.utexas.edu/uttex/) at the University of Texas at Austin, I work with Dr. Sebastian Gomez to determine the redshift range at which the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will be able to observe superluminous supernovae (SLSNe) given its instrumental limitations. Using Python, I analyze low-redshift superluminous supernova (SLSN) light curves from Dr. Gomez's catalog and extend this analysis to higher redshifts to model their detectability using Roman's filter effective area curves.

Comparing the predicted SLSN population with the population Roman will be able to detect will help constrain the rate of SLSNe across cosmic time. Since supernovae are key probes of star formation and early galaxy evolution, deviations between predicted and detectable populations may help determine whether star formation rates at these epochs are higher or lower than expected. This work could ultimately improve our models of star formation and our understanding of early galaxy evolution.

Preliminary results have been obtained and are being refined as additional parameters are incorporated.

## Other Research Experience

In addition to my work on supernovae and massive star evolution, I have participated in several projects involving black hole detection, JWST imaging, and AGN metallicity. With Dr. Karl Gebhardt, I have collected and analyzed spectroscopic data from nearby galaxies and globular clusters to investigate velocity dispersions and search for signatures of central black holes. My current work focuses on measuring stellar velocity dispersion in the nucleus of M88 using Python-based spectral analysis and stellar template fitting. Previously, I examined stellar velocity dispersion profiles and spectral residuals in nearby spiral galaxies and globular clusters to investigate possible central black hole signatures.

I have also worked with JWST galaxy cluster imaging through the VENUS Survey, manually masking stellar diffraction spikes that obscured galaxies. Additionally, I investigated the relationship between AGN metallicity, redshift, and galaxy type through spectroscopic analysis of SDSS and CEERS data. These projects have provided experience with astronomical imaging, spectroscopy, emission-line fitting, and stellar kinematics.

My observational experience at McDonald Observatory complements my computational research. I have independently operated both the 2.1-meter and 2.7-meter telescopes, collecting spectroscopic data for stars, galaxies, globular clusters, supernovae, and tidal disruption events. I have also mentored other undergraduate observers and written telescope operation manuals to support future observing runs.
